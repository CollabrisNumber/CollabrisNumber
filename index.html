Collabris Number Calculator 202610201701

The Collabris number (CN) shows how many co-authorship steps separate any two scientists. It generalises the Erdős number: instead of measuring everyone's distance to one fixed person, it measures the distance between the two people you choose, using the open scholarly index OpenAlex.

How the calculator works

It searches from both people at once: two breadth-first searches grow outward, one co-authorship step at a time, always widening the smaller side, until they meet in the middle. Every step is finished before the meeting is judged, so every shortest chain in the network searched is found. The results are exact only for the co-authorship graph built from the OpenAlex records successfully retrieved, with OpenAlex's author identities and the work types counted; missing or wrongly assigned records can change them.

What the results mean

Collabris number (CN)
The smallest number of co-authorship steps between Person A and Person B. CN = 0 means the two are the same person, CN = 1 means they published together, CN = 2 means they share a co-author, and so on. This is the main result. Every other figure only compares pairs with the same CN.
Newman distance
How close the two are when collaborations are weighted by strength, following Newman (2001). Each joint work with n authors adds 1/(n−1) to the tie strength of every pair of its authors. n is the number of distinct people on the OpenAlex author list, counted after the same identity merging the search uses (records you ticked for one person count once). So the quality of OpenAlex's author identities affects not only whether a link exists but also its strength: a person split across two records makes n one larger, and two people wrongly merged make it one smaller. A link's length is 1 ÷ its strength, and a chain's Newman distance is the sum of its link lengths. The page reports the lowest value among all chains with the fewest steps. Lower means closer.
Vintage
The first year a chain of this length between the two existed. For each link it takes the year of that pair's first joint work. A chain only exists once all its links do, so the chain dates from its latest link. The pair's vintage is the earliest such year across all shortest chains. For direct co-authors it is the year of their first joint publication. Joint works without a publication year are never ignored: such a link could be older than its first dated work, so a chain containing it is known only to be "no later than" its dated links, and a link with no dated work at all leaves its chain's year unknown. The pair's vintage is shown as a year only when every possibility gives the same answer; otherwise as "≤ year" (an upper bound), or "–" when it cannot be bounded.
Chains of people
How many different sequences of people of the minimum length connect the two. Each chain counts once, however many papers support its links. Two chains count as different if they differ in at least one person; they can still share other people and links, so the count is not a number of independent routes. A pair with many chains is usually more robustly connected than a pair with one, but removing a single person or link shared by all chains can still cut every one of them. Shown for CN 2 and above; direct co-authors have exactly one chain, so the page shows their joint publications instead.
Paper routes
The same chains counted paper by paper: each route picks one joint work for every link, so a link with 3 joint works triples the count. For a 2-step chain through a colleague who wrote 2 papers with Person A and 3 with Person B, it is 2 × 3 = 6. Routes through different people are added up. The number grows fast when a chain passes through a very prolific co-author, so compare pairs by chains of people first.

The search, step by step

  1. Find the two people. Each name, ORCID or OpenAlex ID is looked up in OpenAlex. One person can be split across several OpenAlex records, so tick all of them: the ticked records are treated as one person, so their joint papers with each co-author are added up into one link and a chain through them is counted once. If the same record is ticked for both people, they are the same person and CN = 0. People further along a chain are taken as OpenAlex holds them. Someone split across several OpenAlex records counts as several people. That can only make the CN and the Newman distance too high, never too low.
  2. Search from both ends at once. The calculator runs a two-way (bidirectional) breadth-first search. It keeps two growing circles of people: everyone within a certain number of steps of Person A, and everyone within a certain number of steps of Person B. At each step it widens one circle by one co-authorship step: it fetches all the works of the people on that circle's edge from OpenAlex and records every co-author as a person one step further out. It always widens the circle whose edge holds fewer people, because that costs fewer requests; when both edges are the same size, the circle with the smaller radius goes first. Every person on the edge is read before the step ends. Up to 50 OpenAlex records are queried in each request, with up to 100 works returned per page, and up to three requests in flight at a time. Without an API key OpenAlex returns at most 10,000 works per query, so a larger query is split: a group of people into halves, and one person with more than 10,000 works into ranges of publication years. With a key, results are read with a cursor and need no splitting.
  3. Why two ways. A one-way search to a person 4 steps away must read everyone within 3 steps of the start, which for a well-connected scientist can be hundreds of thousands of people. Two circles of radius 2 each meet in the middle after reading only the people within 1 or 2 steps of either end, typically a tiny fraction of that. The answer is the same; only the cost differs.
  4. Stop when the circles meet. As soon as someone is inside both circles, a chain has been found; its length is that person's distance from Person A plus their distance from Person B, and the CN is the smallest such sum. The step that found it is always finished, and both circles are complete up to their radius, so every chain of the minimum length passes through the meeting zone: the first meeting is guaranteed to be a shortest chain. The Newman distance, vintage and chain counts are then exact conditional on the records retrieved: they describe the co-authorship graph built from the OpenAlex works successfully received in this run, with OpenAlex's author identities (records ticked for one person merged, nothing else) and the work types chosen under Options. They are not a statement about the true collaboration record, which OpenAlex may hold incompletely or with author records split or merged wrongly. For every query, the number of works received is checked against the number OpenAlex reports; if any are missing, the result says the search was incomplete and shows the CN as an upper bound (“CN ≤ 3”), because missing works could only add links. Matching counts are a strong sign that nothing was lost, not a proof: works OpenAlex does not count are not seen either. OpenAlex list results show only the first 100 authors of a work, so a work showing 100 authors is read again on its own to get its full author list. If one circle stops growing before the two meet, the two people are not connected in OpenAlex at all. If the two circles' radii add up to the “Give up beyond CN” limit (6 by default) without meeting, the search stops and reports that no chain was found up to that length.
  5. Check records that share an ORCID (diagnostic). The search is then repeated with OpenAlex author records that carry the same ORCID treated as one person, the two searched people's included. If an ORCID learnt during the search shows that two people already in the network are one person, the check is run again from the start with those records merged (replies already received are reused), up to six times; if conflicts remain after that, the check is marked approximate. These merges are not used for the reported numbers, because OpenAlex sometimes attaches an ORCID to the wrong person's record, which would merge two people and make the numbers too low. If the merged search gives a lower CN, a lower Newman distance or an earlier vintage, the page says so and links the records concerned, so they can be checked by hand. The check searches no deeper than the first answer, and has its own request allowance equal to the budget.
  6. Rank the chains. While searching, the page records how many works every pair shares, their Newman strength and their first joint year. Among the shortest chains it then finds the lowest Newman distance and the earliest vintage, and lists chains ordered by Newman distance.

Sources

  • Everything: OpenAlex, an open index of scholarly works, queried live from your browser. Nothing is sent anywhere except OpenAlex.
  • Counting rules: by default, journal articles, book chapters and proceedings papers count. OpenAlex records typed as preprints, errata, datasets, front matter (paratext), peer reviews, retractions or supplementary material are left out, and so are likely meeting abstracts. Choosing “All OpenAlex records” under Options › Definition counts all of them. OpenAlex has no record type for meeting abstracts (they are filed as articles), so they are recognised by their supplement-style pagination - congress pages such as C123 or s45, abstract pages such as 123a, or a supplement issue (S1, Suppl). This is a heuristic: an occasional abstract may slip through, and an occasional full paper in a supplement issue may be left out.

What counts as a collaboration

The Collabris number is meant to say “they were in the room”: two people did the work and signed it together. A journal publication meets that test. A joint dataset deposition, a preprint that duplicates a later paper, or a meeting abstract often does not, or would count the same collaboration twice. So the standard Collabris number counts publications only: journal articles, book chapters and proceedings papers.

Data version and reproducibility

Every connection is resolved against OpenAlex, which is re-indexed continually, so a result is reproducible only relative to the OpenAlex data available at the time of computation. A CN is therefore a statement about the OpenAlex of a particular day. Both downloads record the retrieval window and the calculator version, so a published number can be audited later against the data it rested on.

Compared with the Erdős number

The Collabris number borrows its idea from the Erdős number, which counts co-authorship steps from the mathematician Paul Erdős (1913–1996). The term was first used in print by Casper Goffman in 1969. An Erdős number is a Collabris number with Person B fixed as Erdős; the American Mathematical Society's collaboration-distance tool does the same for any two mathematicians within Mathematical Reviews.

What is the same. Both are the number of steps in the shortest co-authorship chain: 0 for the same person, 1 for co-authors, 2 for co-authors of co-authors. A joint paper creates a link however many authors it has.

What is different.

  • Team size. Mathematics papers usually have few authors. In the experimental sciences a paper can have dozens or thousands, and one large paper can link many people who hardly worked together. That is why this calculator adds the Newman distance: it gives those links little weight, much as the Erdős Number Project's two-author-only variant does, but without discarding them.
  • Data and scope. Erdős numbers are normally computed within Mathematical Reviews, so they cover mathematics well and other fields poorly. The Collabris number uses OpenAlex, which covers all fields, but its author records are generated automatically and are less carefully checked. Please check the linked papers on a surprising chain.
  • Typical values. Among mathematicians with an Erdős number, the median is 5 (mean 4.65, Erdős Number Project, 2004 data). Across all of science, most pairs of active researchers are a handful of steps apart, which is why the default search limit is 6.
  • Extra measures. Erdős numbers are usually reported as a single number. The Collabris number also reports Newman distance, vintage and the number of shortest chains (of people and of papers), so pairs with the same CN can be ranked.

Limitations

  • Author records: OpenAlex sometimes merges two people into one record or splits one person into several. A wrong merge can create a chain that does not really exist, and a split can hide one. Records that merely share an ORCID are not merged for the reported numbers; the ORCID check shows what such merges would change. Follow the linked papers and records on a chain to check it.
  • Exactness is conditional. All shortest chains and their statistics are exact for the graph built from the records successfully retrieved, under OpenAlex's author identities and the chosen work types — not for the real-world collaboration network.
  • Large-team papers count fully for the Collabris number, as in the Erdős number convention, but add almost nothing to Newman strength. Skipping them under Options speeds up the search but may overestimate the CN.
  • The Newman distance is the minimum over shortest chains. A longer chain through much stronger ties can have a lower total. When the people searched include one, the page mentions it, but the true unrestricted minimum would require searching far more of the network.
